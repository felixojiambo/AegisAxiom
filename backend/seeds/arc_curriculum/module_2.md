# Module 2: Identifying the AI Opportunity — The 3 Diagnostic Lenses

## Why you need a framework

Most sales reps in the AI space have the same problem: they walk into a discovery call, the prospect describes a dozen pain points, and the rep has no structured way to figure out which of those pains are actually AI opportunities. The result is usually one of two failure modes. Either the rep promises automation for everything (and ends up over-scoping a project that can't be delivered), or they get vague and say "we can probably help with some of that" (and lose the deal to a competitor who sounds more confident).

The 3 Diagnostic Lenses fix this. They're a fast, repeatable way to listen to a client describe their work and immediately classify which pains are good fits for AI agents — and which aren't.

The three lenses are: **Repeated Workflows**, **Data-Heavy Workflows**, and **Bottleneck Workflows**. Most automation opportunities fall into one of these three categories. A few fall into more than one, and that's actually a good sign — overlapping lenses usually mean the workflow is a high-value target.

## Lens 1: Repeated Workflows

A Repeated Workflow is the simplest case. It's high frequency, low variance, and it can be drawn as a decision tree with a small number of predictable branches. Think: an SDR copying lead info from emails into Salesforce 200 times a day. An AP clerk keying purchase orders into NetSuite. A support agent triaging tickets by routing them to the right queue based on keywords.

The Power Question for Repeated Workflows is: *can I draw this as a flowchart with fewer than 20 boxes, and would it look the same way every time?* If yes, it's a repeated workflow and it's prime AI territory. The marker isn't volume alone — it's the combination of high frequency AND deterministic outcomes. A monthly report that takes 10 minutes isn't a great target (too low frequency). A daily 4-hour task with predictable structure is excellent.

Common trigger phrases to listen for: "we do this every day," "every time a new order comes in," "the same thing, over and over," "we have a checklist for this."

## Lens 2: Data-Heavy Workflows

Data-Heavy Workflows are about cognitive load, not volume. The defining feature is that humans are reading, comparing, and synthesizing large amounts of information across multiple sources to produce a summary or decision. Think: a finance analyst who pulls data from QuickBooks, payroll, and an expense tool every Monday to build a board report. A QA team that reads inspection reports against shipment records to find discrepancies. A sales engineer who reviews 90-minute call transcripts and writes a CRM summary.

The Power Question for Data-Heavy Workflows is: *how much of this work is the human reading and synthesizing, vs. doing or deciding?* If the answer is "most of it," it's data-heavy. The work an LLM does best is exactly this kind of cross-source synthesis — turning unstructured input into structured output. Other useful Power Questions for confirming a Data-Heavy diagnosis: "How many hours per week does this take?", "Are these systems all accessible via API or only through manual exports?", "What format is the final output — is it always the same structure?"

Common trigger phrases: "we spend hours reviewing," "we have to cross-reference," "we pull data from multiple systems," "we summarize this for leadership."

## Lens 3: Bottleneck Workflows

Bottleneck Workflows are different from the other two. The actual work isn't necessarily the problem — the WAITING is. Something is stalling because it depends on a single person, a single approval, a single review step that creates latency for everyone downstream. Think: a sales pipeline that pauses every Friday until the legal lead approves new contract templates. A customer onboarding that waits a week for the compliance officer's questionnaire review. An engineering sprint that can't start until the manager estimates every ticket.

The Power Question for Bottleneck Workflows is: *what's the gap between when work could happen and when it actually happens, and why?* If the gap is "we're waiting on someone," that's a bottleneck. AI bottleneck-busters resolve these by executing rapid pre-reviews, routing context-rich approvals instantly, or autonomously executing decisions within mathematically defined confidence thresholds.

Common trigger phrases: "we're waiting on," "Sarah's the only one who can," "this gets stuck for days," "we can't move forward until."

## First-principles thinking

There's one more skill you need before the lenses become useful: stripping away the human element to look at raw mechanics.

When a client describes a workflow, they describe it the way it currently runs — with all the human handoffs, all the routing, all the emotional context. Your job is to mentally erase the humans and ask: *what are the actual inputs, transformations, and outputs?* When you do this, you usually discover something surprising: a huge amount of "human work" is actually just routing information from one place to another. A person reads an email, decides which department it goes to, and forwards it. That's not judgment — that's pattern matching with two hops.

Once you see how much of human labor is routing rather than judgment, the world looks different. Bottlenecks that seemed like staffing problems reveal themselves as automation opportunities. "Specialized knowledge" that seemed irreplaceable turns out to be a small decision tree the expert has internalized.

This first-principles habit is what separates AI Recruiters who close deals from reps who get stuck listening to clients describe their work the same way the clients describe it. Clients rarely see their own workflows clearly. Your value is seeing them clearly enough to propose the change.

## Putting it together

In a real discovery call, you'll often hear pains that fit more than one lens. Invoice reconciliation is both Repeated AND Data-Heavy. Customer onboarding is often Bottleneck AND Data-Heavy. That's fine — when a workflow lights up multiple lenses, it usually means high MMI score (which you'll learn to calculate in Module 6) and high client urgency.

The goal of Module 2 isn't to file every pain into exactly one box. It's to give you a fast mental sort so that within the first 15 minutes of a discovery call, you can already spot the 3-5 workflows worth digging into on Call 2. That's a superpower in this market. Use it.
